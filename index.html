Independent Researcher · Wilfrid Laurier University · University of Guelph

Abstract

Customer-facing AI assistants now answer questions, quote prices and increasingly act on customers' behalf. In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149), a tribunal held an airline responsible for a fare policy its chatbot had misstated. Yet little is known about how the firms deploying these assistants address AI-related errors in their customer contracts.

We examined 560 large U.S. firms across 93 consumer industries to identify those offering customer-facing AI. For the 133 that offer it, we collected and analyzed 270 contractual and disclosure documents. We coded eleven clause families, distinguishing provisions written specifically for AI from general terms that may also cover it. Two independent LLM-assisted coders coded the corpus, with human validation. We also compared current contracts with versions archived around ChatGPT's November 2022 launch.

Most firms do not expressly address their AI in their contracts: 77 of 133 (58%) have no AI-specific clause in incorporated terms, and only 14 name their assistant. Where AI-specific language exists, it chiefly disclaims accuracy, limits reliance or shifts responsibility to customers. Five firms expressly reserve the right not to honor AI-stated prices or terms, while only one offers limited reimbursement for incorrect AI advice.

Method

  1. Scope. A deployer offers, under its own brand, an AI system that converses with customers by chat or voice or acts on their behalf. AI that customers do not interact with, such as credit scoring, fraud detection or ad targeting, is excluded, as are foundation-model developers and contracts between firms and their AI vendors.
  2. Document collection. Starting from the AI feature itself, we followed the terms linked from the chat window or app, the firm's legal pages, and any agreements the site terms incorporate. These include product or service terms, site terms, dedicated AI terms, in-product disclaimers and, for airlines, contracts of carriage.
  3. Incorporation. Each document is classed by whether, by its own stated scope, it is part of the customer's contract for the channel where the AI operates. This records what the documents claim, not whether a court would enforce them.
  4. Coding. Each document is coded for eleven clause families. Each family is recorded as AI-specific (written for the AI), general (written for the firm's services as a whole), or absent after a full read.
  5. Archival comparison. Current documents are compared sentence by sentence with Wayback Machine versions from around ChatGPT's launch, to separate language written for AI from inherited boilerplate.

Where AI Language Sits

The same protective language can appear in very different places, and whether it is part of the customer's contract depends on where it sits. Each firm is placed in one of four locus categories:

Headline Finding

Most firms do not expressly address their AI in their contracts. 77 of the 133 firms (58%) have no AI-specific clause in any incorporated document, although all of them deploy a live customer-facing assistant. Among the 30 firms whose assistants can act on customers' behalf, 14 have none.

Next Steps

  • Matched EU and UK comparison. The same protocol applied to comparable firms whose consumer contracts are subject to unfair-terms law, to show how much of the pattern reflects U.S. law.
  • Terms versus practice. Comparing these contracts with how firms actually handle AI errors.
  • Tracking change. Using the dataset as a baseline for whether contracts adapt as assistants gain the ability to take action.

Data & Materials

The materials behind the paper are public in the materials folder of this repository (data frozen 14 October 2026). They contain:

  • Dataset. One record per document, with incorporation status, assent mechanism, where the AI language sits, and the code for each of the eleven clause families, plus the firm-level taxonomy.
  • Document register. For every document: URL, retrieval date, stated effective date, capture method, SHA-256 hash, the coding-relevant extract and the coder's note.
  • November 2022 baselines. The archived snapshot used for each comparison, the reason any document has none, and the sentence-level comparison output.
  • Screening log. Every firm screened, with its industry rank, revenue source, decision, reason and evidence link.
  • Coding manual and codebook.
  • Analysis scripts. Sample construction, the archival comparison, the firm-level taxonomy, the segment comparisons and the figures.
  • Reliability data. The second LLM coder's codes for the whole corpus and the human validation codes, with agreement reports.

The company documents themselves are copyrighted by the companies that publish them, so the repository gives each document's URL, archived-snapshot link and hash rather than its full text. Full captured texts are available to researchers on request.
